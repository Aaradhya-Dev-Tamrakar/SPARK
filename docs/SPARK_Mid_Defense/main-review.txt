                                      Abstract

SPARK currently provides offline human-fall model artifacts and tested gateway software,
while its physical wearable pipeline remains incomplete. The intended system combines a
threshold gate and quantized convolutional classifier on an ESP32-S3 with local incident
storage, explanation, and PDF reporting on a laptop gateway. A display client consumes
gateway records rather than performing BLE reception or explanation on the phone.

The existing model preparation uses 100 Hz, two-second windows of 200 samples and
six channels. Archived August evaluation reports give 87.81% floating-point sensitivity
and 0.9185 AUC-ROC on 6,712 held-out windows; the corresponding INT8 report gives
87.43% sensitivity and 0.8717 AUC-ROC. A separate October Colab log reports 89.81%
sensitivity and 0.9272 AUC-ROC, with unresolved threshold provenance. These are archived
experimental records inspected for this report, not rerun evaluations or physical wearable
results.

Driver, interpreter, and transport stubs prevent a claim of integrated MCU operation.
Hardware timing, battery endurance, and approved Nepal cohort results remain unavailable.
This progress report records implementation boundaries, separates model generations, and
replaces calendar schedules with dependency gates. Human fall detection remains the
completion objective; a planned forty-episode MuJoCo study appears only as an exploratory
robotics appendix.

1.1 Problem and motivation

SPARK investigates whether a local wearable-and-gateway system can detect human falls
and preserve inspectable incident records. The engineering problem includes false alarms
during daily activities, incomplete sensor data, limited wearable resources, and the gap
between an offline classifier and an integrated device. A classification score alone cannot
establish that a complete fall event is detected or delivered to a caregiver.

The intended wearable observes six inertial channels and performs gated classification. The
gateway stores event records and prepares explanations and reports locally. This architecture
motivates experiments on computation, detection quality, and evidence traceability; it does
not establish clinical effectiveness or superiority over commercial systems.


1.2 Potential applications

If the integrated design is later validated, a wearable alert could support follow-up for older
adults or other people at fall risk in settings where a fixed room sensor is unavailable. A local
gateway could preserve event records for review and a display client could present alerts to
an authorized caregiver. These are intended use cases only. The present software does not
contact emergency services, continuously track location, or provide a medical diagnosis. The
proposal’s broader aquatic and robot applications are deferred and are not evidence-backed
extensions of the human fall model.


1.3 Objectives and research questions

The primary objective is to complete and evaluate human fall detection. The implementation
must recover an explicit sampling, units, and model-input contract; replace hardware
interfaces with real acquisition and inference; and evaluate event detection after approval of
the cohort protocol.

   1. Can the implemented gate retain fall events while limiting classifier invocations during
      non-fall exposure?

   2. What sensitivity, false-alarm rate, timing, and quantization loss occur under a frozen
      subject/episode holdout?

   3. Can the wearable, gateway, and display preserve event identity and provenance through
      the reporting pipeline?


                                               1
   4. What limitations arise when transferring waist-worn public data to the proposed
      dorsal-wrist placement?


1.4 Scope and limitations

The evidence presently concerns SisFall-derived offline windows, stored model outputs,
source inspection, and software tests. The proposed Nepal cohort is not a collected dataset.
A protocol draft does not constitute institutional approval. Young volunteer simulations
would not, by themselves, validate performance in frail older adults or natural falls.

Robotic sensing, emergency-stop interfaces, active fall mitigation, aquatic monitoring, and
physical robot experiments are out of scope for the human completion milestone. Appendix B
defines an unexecuted experiment rather than an implemented capability. No first-in-literature
or production-readiness claim is made.


1.5 Evidence conventions

Current inspection means directly observed file contents, artifact identity, array metadata,
or checks executed for this preparation. Archived measured results means numerical
outputs retained from an earlier experiment and not independently rerun here. Historical
reported results means tracker or document narratives without a corresponding raw run
record inspected in this preparation. Estimates require assumptions; planned work requires
completion evidence before becoming a result.

The appendix evidence register maps claims to repository paths and the local snapshots in
this report folder. Source existence does not prove physical deployment. Result-dependent
sections remain pending; defense dates and procurement dates are not inferred from old
schedules.

                Chapter 2: Background and Related Work


2.1 Fall detection and application context

Falls are an important source of injury and loss of independence among older adults. A
wearable detector is intended to identify a suspected event when a person is away from
fixed cameras or room sensors, preserve a traceable event record, and support a caregiver’s
follow-up. Such a detector is an assistive alerting tool; it does not diagnose injury, replace
emergency services, or establish that a person is safe when no alarm occurs. Global context
motivates the research, but local prevalence and demand are not measured by this project [1].

The research literature spans threshold rules, feature-based classifiers, temporal neural
networks, edge inference, and systems that send candidate signals to a server. Each choice
affects more than classification accuracy: sensor placement, event definitions, false alarms
over ordinary activity, compute and radio duty, notification delay, usability, and evaluation
population all matter. SisFall is a public benchmark for reproducible development, but its
waist-mounted signals and largely acted falls do not validate a dorsal-wrist system or natural
falls [2].


2.2 Comparative literature review

Threshold methods are computationally simple and interpretable. Kangas et al. compared
low-complexity algorithms at waist, wrist, and head placements; their results emphasize
that the best site and rule depend on placement, and that the wrist signal poses a particular
challenge [3]. Bourke et al. evaluated tri-axial thresholds using simulated falls and older
adults’ daily activities, illustrating both the value of trunk signals and the limits of transferring
a threshold across sensor positions [4]. These studies motivate a gate as a candidate filter,
not the assumption that a fixed gate preserves every fall.

Learned models can represent temporal patterns that a single magnitude rule cannot. SisFall
provides 15 fall activities and 19 activities of daily living from 38 participants, with inertial
sensors; it is a useful public source for offline model development, subject-grouped evaluation,
and comparison of preprocessing choices [2]. CNN and recurrent approaches represent
temporal structure differently. Nho et al.’s cited title and the listed Wang et al. citation could
not be matched to authoritative primary records using the supplied metadata; this report
therefore does not repeat their performance claims or use them to rank architectures. Those
entries remain visible in the proposal-reference audit.

Embedded inference literature establishes a feasible runtime route, not a guarantee for
this board and model. TensorFlow Lite Micro describes an interpreter and evaluation for

constrained embedded systems; actual RAM, timing, operator coverage, and whole-firmware
size still require target measurements [5]. Xu et al. provide a directly relevant hybrid example:
their wearable threshold stage forwards candidate acceleration windows to a server-side CNN,
so the classifier is not fully local to the wearable [6]. Yu et al. report a TinyCNN wearable

Other system designs broaden the comparison beyond classifier topology. Tseng et al.
describe a finite-state wearable detector with GPS, NB-IoT, and notifications, demonstrating
a location-aware communication path distinct from SPARK’s local gateway design [9].
Bhattacharyya et al. describe a CNN-kNN fall detector on a RISC-V system implemented
on FPGA; this is an accelerator-oriented hardware point of comparison, not evidence that a
low-cost MCU has equivalent resources [10]. Baños et al.’s mHealthDroid is relevant to
mobile-health framework context, but SPARK’s phone client is deliberately display-only and
does not adopt its broader sensor integration architecture [11].

Explainability terminology also constrains the comparison. SHAP defines additive feature
attributions grounded in Shapley values [12]. The SPARK gateway’s input-times-gradient
calculation is a different attribution method, while the live v1 event’s peak-feature display is
a heuristic summary because the packet does not contain a waveform. Neither should be
labeled SHAP. Yao et al. study ECG heartbeat interpretation using CNN and GRU; that
biomedical time-series example supplies context but is not fall-detection evidence [13]. Liu et
al.’s IEEE conference paper concerns explainable wearable human-activity recognition rather
than fall-event explanation [14]. Kundu et al.’s review is an arXiv preprint submitted in 2026
(arXiv:2604.09799), rather than a 2025 peer-reviewed article as the proposal bibliography
says [15].


2.3 Hardware and software theory

An inertial measurement unit combines accelerometer and angular-rate measurements. A
three-axis accelerometer observes specific force in its sensor frame; a gyroscope observes
angular velocity. Orientation, gravity separation, scale factors, axis alignment, and units
must be considered before thresholds or models trained on one representation are applied
to another. The current preprocessing selection retains raw SisFall sensor counts, whereas
the event protocol describes peak values in g and degrees per second. The exact transform
linking those representations is unresolved and must be specified before firmware integration.

2.4 Interpretation and study position

SPARK’s defensible present contribution is an inspectable software pipeline, offline model
artifacts, and an evaluation design that separates archived results from future integrated
measurements. The literature supports testing threshold filtering, quantized embedded
inference, event traceability, and local explanation as design questions. It does not establish
novelty, clinical suitability, power savings, or superiority for this system. The exact source
records and claim-level disposition for all twenty proposal references are documented in
docs/thesis_work/PROPOSAL_COVERAGE_AND_REFERENCE_AUDIT.md.

3.2 Sampling and input contract

Let 𝑓𝑠 be sampling frequency and 𝑁 the number of samples. The nominal window duration
is 𝑇 = 𝑁/ 𝑓𝑠 . Existing preprocessing sets 𝑓𝑠 = 100 Hz and 𝑁 = 200, hence 𝑇 = 2 s. Model
arrays have six channels ordered as acceleration then angular-rate axes. The planned logger
targets 200 Hz acquisition; it is not evidence that the model was trained at that rate.

A 200-sample stream at 200 Hz has one second of coverage. A three-second 200 Hz input
requires 600 samples. Consequently, the historical combination “200 Hz, three seconds,
200×6” is rejected. This report retains the executable model convention and requires a
versioned resampling adapter for any higher-rate logger. It does not silently alter the trained
model.

The source preparation retains sensor counts in the selected columns; it does not establish
a calibrated physical-unit conversion. Before connecting firmware, reconcile accelerom-
eter/gyro scaling, axis signs, normalization, interpolation, anti-aliasing, and quantization.
Timestamp gaps, clipping, and non-finite input must be explicit rejection or quality flags.

3.4 Holdout and evaluation design

The training source uses grouped holdouts and asserts subject-set disjointness. Group-based
fractions need not yield identical fractions of windows, and the helper is not repeated
cross-validation. The first source split reserves a test group fraction of 0.2; the second split
uses a validation fraction of 0.1/0.9 within the retained groups. Do not describe this code as
a guaranteed 80/10/10 window split. The October notebook run must be traced separately
from the CLI source.

Retain exact subject/episode IDs, class counts, seeds, preprocessing revision, and hashes for
each split. Trial-level labels inherited by every window can include pre/post-fall periods;
event-level ground truth must eventually resolve onset and contact rather than treating every
such window as a separate fall.

The October run describes asymmetric focal loss. In general binary form,

3.8 Gated-pipeline analysis

For annotated fall events let 𝐺 mean the gate admits the event and 𝐶 mean the classifier
detects it. The pipeline sensitivity obeys

                           𝑃(𝐺 ∩ 𝐶 | 𝐹) = 𝑃(𝐺 | 𝐹)𝑃(𝐶 | 𝐺, 𝐹).

Classifier recall measured only on admitted events is conditional recall; it does not reveal
gate misses. This identity motivates separate gate-only, classifier-only, and end-to-end results
on the same event manifest. It is an analytical relation, not an empirical SPARK performance
estimate.

Define inference duty fraction as classifier invocations divided by eligible window opportu-
nities under a frozen stride and gate policy. Reducing this count may reduce computation,
but battery savings require measured current and acquisition/radio duty cycles. A lower duty
fraction must be interpreted alongside missed falls and alarm timing.

B.1 Motion source and feasibility gates

A proposed motion source is the Gymnasium Humanoid-v5 model in MuJoCo. Official
documentation exposes a custom xml_file and configurable unhealthy termination [16].
MuJoCo supplies accelerometer and gyro sensors aligned to an MJCF site [17]. These
interfaces support documentary feasibility, not a tested SPARK simulator integration.

Before generating data, pin the simulator, environment, MJCF and controller revisions,
integrator, timestep, contact settings, and seeds. A reproducible controller must demonstrate
stable walking and recovery on development-only seeds; the stock environment is not itself
a validated locomotion controller or a fall dataset. Random actions do not satisfy the
stable-locomotion condition. Verify controller licensing and provenance separately.

The inspected model timestep is 0.003 s and the environment defaults to five physics steps
per action, implying 0.015 s between actions [18, 19]. Source and runtime settings must
prevail over inconsistent timing prose in documentation. Instrument physics substeps;
an observation once per action is insufficient for the proposed 200 Hz archive. Set
terminate_when_unhealthy=False and an explicit 30 s episode limit to retain contact
and recovery. Reject non-finite or unstable trajectories. No simulator was installed or run for
this report.
