\chapter{\scshape Literature Review}

Fall detection using wearable inertial sensors has been an active research area for over two decades. The literature can be categorized across multiple architectural and computational axes: threshold-based methods, classical machine learning, always-on edge neural networks, distributed server-offloaded gating, hardware-interrupt gating, and emerging explainable AI (XAI) frameworks.

\textbf{Threshold-based methods} compute a scalar magnitude from accelerometer axes --- typically the resultant $|a| = \sqrt{a_x^2 + a_y^2 + a_z^2}$ --- and trigger a fall event when a spike followed by a quiet period is detected. Kangas et al.\ \cite{kangas2008comparison} compared multiple threshold algorithms on hip and wrist IMU data and reported sensitivity values of 73--95\% depending on placement, but with false-positive rates exceeding 30\% on vigorous ADLs. Bourke and Lyons \cite{bourke2008threshold} proposed a dual-axis threshold method achieving 96\% sensitivity, though specificity on sit-to-stand transitions remained poor. The fundamental limitation of threshold-only approaches is that rapid voluntary motions (e.g., sitting down quickly, stumbling without falling) trigger the threshold, forcing an intractable trade-off between sensitivity and false alarms.

\textbf{Machine learning classifiers} applied to hand-crafted time- and frequency-domain features from IMU windows addressed several threshold limitations. Sucerquia et al.\ published the SisFall dataset \cite{sucerquia2017sisfall}, a standard benchmark containing 15 fall types and 19 ADL types from 38 subjects. Classical classifiers (SVM, Random Forest) trained on SisFall features achieved sensitivity above 90\%, but were evaluated offline or on high-power host computers rather than wearable microcontrollers.

\textbf{Always-on edge deep learning} has eliminated manual feature engineering by feeding raw multi-axis IMU streams directly into embedded neural networks. David et al.\ \cite{david2021tensorflow} demonstrated the viability of INT8-quantized CNNs under 320\,KB RAM using TensorFlow Lite Micro. Recent works have targeted wearable microcontrollers directly: Yu et al.\ \cite{yu2023tinycnn} introduced TinyCNN running continuously on an Arduino Nano 33 BLE Sense (37\,ms latency, $>98\%$ specificity), while Hu et al.\ \cite{hu2025microfallnet} developed MicroFallNet on ESP32-S3 (14\,ms latency, 9.6\,KB footprint). However, these architectures operate as \textit{always-on} inference engines, executing the neural network continuously across every sliding window regardless of activity, creating unnecessary active-power drain on wearable batteries.

\textbf{Distributed gating vs.\ single-chip execution:} To conserve power, two-stage gating architectures have been proposed, but existing designs split execution across network tiers. Xu et al.\ \cite{xu2021fusion} implemented a threshold-based module (TBM) on an MSP430 wearable node, but explicitly offloaded the subsequent CNN confirmation to a remote server. Other recent works similarly use LoRa or RF links to trigger heavy neural networks or vision models on remote compute nodes. While effective at reducing on-node compute, these distributed schemes introduce critical network latencies and failure points unacceptable in low-connectivity South Asian eldercare environments.

\textbf{Hardware wake vs.\ continuous software ISR gating:} A hardware alternative to continuous software monitoring is the IMU-native wake-on-motion interrupt. Alvarez et al.\ \cite{sensors2025wearable} configured an LSM6DSO IMU in wake-on-shake mode to hold an STM32WB5MM MCU in low-power STOP mode until disturbed. While ultra-low power, hardware interrupts lack temporal duration filtering and suffer from high false wake-ups during arm swings. In contrast, SPARK executes a continuous 200\,Hz software magnitude-and-duration gate within the MCU ISR without sleeping, maintaining complete temporal awareness before invoking the CNN.

\textbf{Custom silicon and FPGA accelerators:} Cross-tier hybrid architectures such as Vigil \cite{vigil2024fpga} combine probabilistic CNNs and k-NN classifiers on custom RISC-V SoCs synthesized on FPGAs. While achieving same-chip multi-stage execution, their hardware complexity and fabrication cost place them far above the sub-\$5 commodity microcontroller tier targeted by SPARK.

\textbf{Explainability in wearable health AI:} Clinical adoption of AI systems demands transparent decision rationale. Lundberg and Lee \cite{lundberg2017shap} established SHAP as a mathematically rigorous attribution framework based on cooperative game theory. In health informatics, SHAP has been applied to ECG classification \cite{yao2021interpretation} and general Human Activity Recognition (HAR). Kundu et al.\ \cite{kundu2025explainable} surveyed XAI in HAR, noting that prior works (e.g., Liu et al.\ \cite{liu2024explainable} using KernelSHAP on CNN-LSTMs, and Arul et al. on XGBoost) perform heavy model-agnostic permutations offline on generic activity classes (walking, standing). No published system executes real-time gradient-based feature attribution ($X \times \nabla_X Y$) on a local companion gateway specifically tailored for clinical fall event audits.

\textbf{Gap Summary \& Academic Novelty:} SPARK addresses these limitations through two core defensible contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{On-Device Gated Pipeline (Claim 1):} Combining a continuous software magnitude-and-duration threshold gate with an INT8 quantized 1D CNN confirmation, both executing entirely on a single sub-\$5 MCU (ESP32-S3) with zero classification offload.
\item \textbf{Local Companion-Gateway XAI (Claim 3):} Real-time gradient attribution ($X \times \nabla_X Y$) and automated 1-page clinical PDF reporting executing locally on a companion gateway laptop with zero cloud dependencies.
\end{enumerate}
